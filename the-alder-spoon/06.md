# The Alder Spoon

## I. The Hollow

The handle is set. The geometry is fixed. Now comes the part where the stick becomes a vessel, and it is here that the alder reveals its secret: it is not solid. It is a sponge waiting to be filled.

To carve a bowl is to negotiate with density. Oak would fight you with its grain, splintering at the edges, demanding you cut across the fiber or suffer the tear. But alder is soft, almost yielding, like cutting into a ripe tomato or cold butter. It does not resist; it absorbs. When I press the edge of my bevel-edge knife into the end grain of the handle, the blade does not slice so much as it sinks. There is a moment of high tension, a micro-second where the wood is deciding whether to give way or hold fast. In alder, it always gives way.

This is the "drinking" sensation. As I make my first crescent cut, shaving away a sliver of the center, the wood pulls the blade in. It is a tactile suction. You feel the grain fibers parting, not snapping. If you push too hard, you lose control; the knife plows a trench too deep, and you have to fight for the surface back. The alder wants to be empty. It wants to become a cup. The challenge is not making it hollow; the challenge is stopping before it becomes a hole.

I work in layers. A spoon bowl is not carved in one sweep; it is a series of diminishing craters. I start with a V-shaped groove down the center, establishing the depth. Then I widen the walls, angling the blade so the cut is shallow and broad. The shavings come off in translucent curls, thin enough to see the light through them, darkening to a reddish-brown at the center where the wood was densest.

There is a specific weight to this process. My hand is not just moving the knife; it is feeling the center of gravity shift. As the wood disappears, the spoon gets lighter, but it also gets top-heavy. I am balancing two opposing forces: the need for depth to hold soup, and the need for mass in the handle to keep the thing from tipping in the bowl. If the bowl is too deep, the spoon feels like a ladle—clumsy, heavy in the tip. If it is too shallow, it is a spoon for dust, useless for substance.

I look for the "sweet spot," a depth of about eighteen millimeters. At this point, the bowl should feel substantial in the palm but light on the tongue. I run my thumb across the curve of the bowl I am forming. It should feel like a small valley, a landscape that guides liquid to the center without creating a reservoir that is hard to pour. The alder is damp now, sticky with its own oils. It feels alive under the blade, warm from the friction.

The most satisfying moment is when the walls of the bowl become thin enough to flex. I press my thumb inside the rim, testing the give. A good spoon bowl should have a slight spring to it, a resilience. If it is stiff, I have left too much wood; the spoon will feel rigid and industrial. If it is flimsy, I have carved too much; it will feel fragile, like a paper cup. I want the "breath" of the wood. I want the sensation that if I lifted this spoon, it would rise with a slight, buoyant hesitation, as if it were half-made of air.

This is where the tactile satisfaction peaks. It is not about the beauty of the curve—though the curve must be smooth, free of chatter marks and ridges. It is about the feedback loop between my thumb and the wood. I can feel the exact thickness of the wall under my nail. I can feel where the wood is thicker on the left side, where my hand drifted, and I shave a micron off the right side to compensate. The alder drinks the pressure of my correction. It evens out.

I am not building a spoon; I am revealing the one that was already there, hiding in the stick. The alder is cooperative. It forgives small mistakes. It holds the water. And when I finally lift the tool away and run my tongue across the inside curve, I taste the sap, the earth, and the precise, perfect balance of weight and depth. It is a small, dark cup, ready to hold the world.

## II. The Finish

The hollow is done. The knife is set aside, and for the first time, I hold the spoon up to the window light. It is ugly. It is covered in tiny, jagged chatter marks where the blade skipped, faint ridges where I hesitated, and a general grayness that speaks of raw, exposed cellulose. It looks like a tool, not an object of desire. But this is where the alder starts to speak back, and the only way to hear it is to smooth the noise.

Sanding is a form of erasure, but it is also a revelation. I start with 80-grit sandpaper, wet, just enough to keep the dust down and let the paper glide. I am not trying to make the surface flat; I am trying to erase the knife. I sand in the direction of the grain, always, feeling the fibers lift and lay flat against the surface. The alder is forgiving here, too. It does not resist the abrasion. It simply thins. I watch the grayness fade, replaced by a pale, creamy yellow. The wood feels cold and dry, like bone.

Then comes the 120-grit, and the 220. With each pass, the surface becomes less of a barrier and more of a membrane. I am looking for the moment when the spoon disappears under my fingertip. When there is no longer a distinction between the wood and the skin, only the warmth of both. The alder has a unique property: it is dense enough to hold its shape, but porous enough to drink. As I sand, I can feel the pores opening up, waiting to be filled.

This is where the grain reveal happens. I take a rag soaked in a mixture of boiled linseed oil and a drop of pure tung oil. I do not pour it on; I press it into the surface. The alder drinks it instantly. The pale yellow darkens to a warm, honeyed amber. The grain, which was invisible under the raw cut, suddenly rises to the surface like a ghost. The earlywood and latewood contrast sharply, creating a pattern of fine, dark lines that ripple across the bowl. It looks like water, or like the rings of a tree stump, or like the topography of a map. The oil seals the fibers, locking the color in, but it also adds a depth that sanding alone could never achieve. It gives the wood a "body."

I let the oil sit for ten minutes, just long enough for it to penetrate the capillaries but not so long that it becomes tacky. I wipe off the excess with a clean, dry cloth. The sound is satisfying: a soft, dry *shhh* as the rag moves across the surface. The spoon is no longer matte. It has a low, soft sheen, like wet stone. The color is deeper now, richer, and the grain is crisp. The chatter marks are gone, smoothed into the landscape. The ridges have dissolved. The spoon looks finished, but I know it is not. It is only resting.

The final step is the handle. I have been careful not to over-oil the grip, as it would become slippery. I use a slightly drier oil, almost a wax, rubbed in with a cloth until the surface is barely glossy. The handle should feel like it belongs in your hand, not on it. It should have a friction that allows for a secure grip, even when wet. I run my thumb up and down the shaft, checking for any rough spots, any places where the oil has pooled. I buff it with a soft piece of chamois, polishing the micro-scratches out of the surface.

Now, I hold it up again. The bowl is a dark, warm cup. The handle is a pale, textured wand. The contrast is striking. The alder has settled into its final form. It is no longer a stick, and it is no longer a rough draft. It is a vessel. The oil has brought out the warmth of the wood, the "breath" I was looking for earlier. The spoon feels lighter now, as if the oil has added a spirit to the matter. It is ready to be used. It is ready to hold the soup, the tea, the silence. It is ready to be held in the hand, to be felt, to be tasted. The alder has given itself to me, and I have given it back to the world, changed.

### Workshop Notes: The Diminishing Craters Technique

**Concept:**
The "diminishing craters" method is a way to manage the removal of material in the spoon bowl without creating deep, hard-to-correct trenches. Instead of carving the entire depth in one pass, you carve a series of nested, smaller bowls, each slightly wider and shallower than the last. This allows you to control the curvature at every stage and keeps the knife edge engaged with the wood rather than plowing through it.

**Steps:**

1.  **The Anchor (Crater 1):**
    *   Locate the center of the spoon end.
    *   Make a small, deep V-shaped cut. This is your anchor. It should be no wider than the thickness of your knife blade.
    *   *Goal:* Establish the deepest point of the bowl.

2.  **The First Expansion (Crater 2):**
    *   Widen the cut, angling the blade outward at roughly 45 degrees.
    *   Remove a ring of wood around the anchor.
    *   *Goal:* Create a small, shallow basin. The walls should be steep but not vertical.

3.  **The Second Expansion (Crater 3):**
    *   Widen the cut again, this time with a shallower angle (closer to 30 degrees).
    *   Remove another ring of wood.
    *   *Goal:* Begin forming the curve of the bowl. The transition from the deep center to the wider edge should be smooth.

4.  **The Rim Formation (Crater 4):**
    *   Widen the cut to the desired final width of the spoon bowl.
    *   Keep the cut very shallow. You are now defining the rim.
    *   *Goal:* Create a smooth, continuous curve from the center to the edge.

5.  **The Refinement:**
    *   Switch to a finer grind or a different knife angle.
    *   Smooth out any ridges or steps between the "craters."
    *   *Goal:* Ensure the bowl feels like one continuous surface, not a series of steps.
