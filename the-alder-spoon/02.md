# The Alder Spoon: Handle Notes

There is a specific moment in the carving of a spoon where the bowl is done, the curve is true, and you are left with nothing but the handle. It is a quieter task, less about shaping a vessel and more about finding the spine of the stick. With alder, the wood is forgiving but it has opinions. It is pale, almost cream-colored, with a grain that runs in subtle, wavy lines rather than the aggressive straightness of maple or the coarse knots of oak.

Holding the knife to the handle, you are not trying to remove material so much as you are trying to align the wood’s memory with your intention. The resistance here is different from the bowl. In the bowl, the wood gives way to the curve; in the handle, you are asking the grain to hold its shape against the friction of the hand. You carve in the direction of the fibers, or the blade will snag and tear. It demands a kind of patience that feels almost architectural. You have to listen to the sound the steel makes against the soft wood—a high, thin *shhh* when it’s smooth, a dull *thud* when you’ve gone too deep or too fast.

The shavings from the handle are delicate. They don’t curl into thick ribbons like they do on the bowl; they are thin, papery flakes that catch the light. They smell of nothing, really, just wet earth and faint sap, a scent that is so subtle it only registers when the room is silent.

This part of the process requires a stillness that is hard to maintain. There is no room for hurry. If you rush, the handle becomes lopsided, or the curve where it meets the bowl feels abrupt, like a seam rather than a continuation. You find yourself breathing out as you pull the knife, letting your exhale guide the depth of the cut. It is a meditative loop: assess, shave, feel, assess again. You are not just making a tool for eating; you are negotiating a physical relationship with a dead tree, trying to make its structure feel inevitable in your palm.

Why alder? It is a choice born of utility and temperament.

In the world of wetware—spoons, ladles, buckets, barrels—alder is the quiet workhorse. It does not have the dense, interlocked fiber structure of cherry or maple, which makes them harder to carve but harder to stain. It lacks the coarse, oily resistance of walnut. Instead, alder offers a specific kind of rot resistance that is not about imperviousness, but about absorption. It is porous enough to take up water, but its cellular structure allows it to dry out relatively evenly without the dramatic checking and splitting that softer, faster-growing woods like basswood often suffer from when subjected to repeated wet-dry cycles. It is a wood that expects to be damp. It is the wood of riverbanks and boggy soil, so it is at home when the bowl holds soup or the handle gets wet from a wash.

But the primary reason for choosing alder is the feel of the blade in the wood. It is soft, but not spongy. It has a "butter" quality to it. When you draw a sharpened gouge through alder, the cut is clean and continuous, not a series of tiny, jagged chugs. It carves with a silence that is satisfying. For a beginner, or even for an experienced carver looking to refine a technique, alder is forgiving. It does not punish a slip of the hand with a catastrophic split. It allows you to correct your path. It is a wood that invites you to keep going, to keep refining, because the feedback is immediate and gentle. It is the difference between chipping away at concrete and smoothing out clay.

Once the shape is true, and the knife work is finished, the process shifts to the surface. This is where the magic of alder reveals itself.

You start with the coarse sanding. A 120-grit paper, held flat, dragging along the grain. You are not trying to make it glass-smooth yet; you are trying to level the high spots left by the knife, to remove the tiny ridges of wood that stood up when the fibers were cut. You feel the roughness change under your fingertips, the surface becoming uniformly resistant. You move to 220, then 320. With each grit, the pale, creamy color of the raw wood begins to shift. The surface becomes smoother, yes, but more importantly, it becomes more receptive. The pores, which were open and thirsty after the knife, begin to close slightly under the abrasion, creating a base that is ready to drink.

Then comes the oil.

I use a simple food-grade mineral oil, or sometimes a blend of mineral oil and beeswax. You apply it with a clean cloth, rubbing it into the wood in the direction of the grain. At first, the oil just sits on the surface, darkening the color slightly. But as you work it in, the alder seems to pull it in. The color deepens from a pale cream to a warm, honeyed tan. The grain, which was subtle and almost invisible before, suddenly emerges. Those wavy lines become distinct, darkening to a rich amber where the oil pools in the slightly softer latewood.

It is a dramatic transformation. The spoon, which looked like a piece of plain, unfinished wood, suddenly looks like itself. It looks warm. It looks like it has history. The oil does not just protect the wood; it brings out the wood. It highlights the density variations, the slight swirls near the end grain, the subtle differences in how the tree grew each year.

You let it sit. You let the excess oil dry, then you wipe it off. The surface is now smooth, not in a plastic way, but in a way that feels like skin. It is ready to be used. And when you hold it, you can feel the warmth of the wood, the slight tackiness of the oil, and the weight of it, balanced and true in your hand. It is no longer just a carved stick. It is a tool. It is a companion.
