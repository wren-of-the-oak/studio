# The Grain of Water

I kept the alder stick in the pantry for three weeks before I touched it. Not out of superstition, but because alder is wet wood. It holds moisture in a way that oak or maple don’t; it’s the wood of swamps and riverbanks, and even the dry end of the spectrum feels damp to the touch. I wanted to feel that dampness before I started shaving it away.

When I finally lifted the blade, the first few strokes were resistant. The fibers were tight, locked together by tension and tannin. The sound was a high, dry whisper, *shhh, shhh*, as the gouge peeled off curls that smelled faintly of sawdust and old paper. There was no color yet. Just pale, creamy gray.

But then, halfway down the bowl, the grain shifted.

Alder doesn’t have a straight grain. It’s chaotic. It twists. In this particular stick, there was a knot just below the handle joint, and the fibers spiraled around it like smoke rising from a candle. As I carved the bowl, I wasn’t just following the contour of the spoon; I was navigating a topography. The wood pushed back here, yielded there. I could feel the direction of the grain under the flat of my thumb, a subtle resistance that told me where the next slice would break clean and where it would tear.

I stopped shaving when the wood began to change temperature.

It’s a specific sensation: the moment the raw, cold timber warms under the friction of the blade. The surface loses its chalky dust and becomes smooth, almost waxy. The pale gray deepens into a soft, honeyed beige. It looks like skin. It looks like the inside of a seashell.

I took the spoon to the sink. Water is the true test of alder. I ran the cold tap over the bowl, and the wood drank it instantly. The color darkened dramatically, turning a rich, amber brown. The grain pattern—those swirling lines around the knot—became visible, bold and dark against the lighter background. It looked like a map of a small, quiet island.

But wet wood is only half the story. The final shift happens when it dries and I apply the oil.

I used a simple tung oil, applied with a rag. As the oil soaked in, the surface didn’t just get shiny; it got deep. The tactile quality changed from smooth to slick. When I ran my finger across the back of the handle, I could feel the micro-undulations of the grain, the slight ridges where the fibers had been compressed. It’s no longer just wood; it’s a landscape you can hold.

The most distinct part is the weight. A carved alder spoon is lighter than a wooden spoon made from hardwood, but it feels dense in the hand. That’s the oil. It fills the pores, adding a subtle mass, a sense of presence. It doesn’t feel like a tool anymore. It feels like an object that has been completed.

I left it on the windowsill for two days to cure. Now, when I pick it up, the first thing I notice is the smell. Not sawdust. Not oil. Just wood. A clean, dry, sweet scent that reminds me of the forest floor in late autumn. It’s a smell that lingers on the fingertips for hours.

The grain is still there, under the oil, waiting to be seen in the light. But the touch is different. It’s intimate. It’s the difference between looking at a tree and holding a piece of its heart.
