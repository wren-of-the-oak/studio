# The Alder Spoon: What the Grain Remembers

The alder stick did not start as wood. It started as a memory of rain.

When I first held it, the bark was still peeling in long, papery strips, revealing the pale, honeyed flesh beneath. It smelled of wet earth and ozone, a scent that lingers in the lungs long after you’ve stopped breathing it in. Alder is a thirsty tree. It loves the damp, the rot, the places where the water table is close to the surface. To carve it is to work with something that has already been soaking up the world.

I sharpened my knife until the edge could split a hair, then I began to shave. The first cuts were tentative, testing the density. Alder is soft, almost yielding, but it has a secret stubbornness. It doesn’t want to be shaped by force; it wants to be coaxed. As I removed the shavings, the grain began to speak. It wasn’t just a pattern; it was a history.

Every ring was a year of standing in the wind. Every slight variation in tone was a season of drought or a summer of heavy rain. I could feel the tree’s rhythm under my fingertips, a pulse of growth and dormancy, expansion and contraction. The wood remembered the sun on its leaves, the cold snap in March, the slow, heavy pull of autumn. By holding the stick, I was holding a timeline, compressed and silent.

Then, the knife hit the knot.

It was a small bump, a swirling chaos of fibers where a branch had once broken off. The edge of my blade caught, snagged, and skipped. I paused, turning the stick over to look at the damage. It was a scar. The tree had healed over the wound, packing new wood over the broken limb, layer upon layer, until the injury was buried but never erased. I didn’t want to smooth it out. I wanted to keep it. I carved carefully around the knot, letting the fibers twist and tangle, creating a natural whorl in the handle. It was a reminder that nothing is perfect, that even in the heart of the tree, there was violence and repair.

Hours passed. The bowl deepened. The handle thinned. The wood became warm from the friction of the knife, and the smell of fresh shavings filled the air, sharp and green.

I wiped the surface with a dry cloth, checking for splinters. The wood was smooth, but not glassy. It had texture, a slight drag against the thumb. I walked to the sink and turned on the tap. I didn’t fill it to the brim; I just let the water run until the bowl was full, a clear, shallow pool.

The first thing I noticed was the weight.

It wasn’t heavy, not like a stone or a metal cup. But it was there, a distinct, cool pressure in the palm. The water settled into the curve of the alder, finding the lowest point of my carving. I tilted my wrist slightly, just a fraction of a degree, and the surface of the water shifted, a ripple moving toward the lip. I tilted it back. The water steadied.

I stood there for a minute, holding the spoon out in front of me, arms steady. I could feel the coolness of the water seeping through the thin walls of the bowl, changing the temperature of the wood against my skin. It was a subtle shift, from the dry warmth of the carving to the damp chill of the liquid. I didn’t drink it. I just felt the specific, physical reality of the water’s mass, held in place by nothing but the integrity of the fibers and the angle of my wrist. It was a test of the structure, a practical verification that the wood was sound. I poured it out, watched the droplets fall back into the sink, and set the spoon on the counter to dry.
