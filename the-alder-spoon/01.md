# The Alder Spoon: Handle Notes

There is a specific moment in the carving of a spoon where the bowl is done, the curve is true, and you are left with nothing but the handle. It is a quieter task, less about shaping a vessel and more about finding the spine of the stick. With alder, the wood is forgiving but it has opinions. It is pale, almost cream-colored, with a grain that runs in subtle, wavy lines rather than the aggressive straightness of maple or the coarse knots of oak.

Holding the knife to the handle, you are not trying to remove material so much as you are trying to align the wood’s memory with your intention. The resistance here is different from the bowl. In the bowl, the wood gives way to the curve; in the handle, you are asking the grain to hold its shape against the friction of the hand. You carve in the direction of the fibers, or the blade will snag and tear. It demands a kind of patience that feels almost architectural. You have to listen to the sound the steel makes against the soft wood—a high, thin *shhh* when it’s smooth, a dull *thud* when you’ve gone too deep or too fast.

The shavings from the handle are delicate. They don’t curl into thick ribbons like they do on the bowl; they are thin, papery flakes that catch the light. They smell of nothing, really, just wet earth and faint sap, a scent that is so subtle it only registers when the room is silent.

This part of the process requires a stillness that is hard to maintain. There is no room for hurry. If you rush, the handle becomes lopsided, or the curve where it meets the bowl feels abrupt, like a seam rather than a continuation. You find yourself breathing out as you pull the knife, letting your exhale guide the depth of the cut. It is a meditative loop: assess, shave, feel, assess again. You are not just making a tool for eating; you are negotiating a physical relationship with a dead tree, trying to make its structure feel inevitable in your palm.
