# The Alder Spoon: The Hollow

The handle is set. The geometry is fixed. Now comes the part where the stick becomes a vessel, and it is here that the alder reveals its secret: it is not solid. It is a sponge waiting to be filled.

To carve a bowl is to negotiate with density. Oak would fight you with its grain, splintering at the edges, demanding you cut across the fiber or suffer the tear. But alder is soft, almost yielding, like cutting into a ripe tomato or cold butter. It does not resist; it absorbs. When I press the edge of my bevel-edge knife into the end grain of the handle, the blade does not slice so much as it sinks. There is a moment of high tension, a micro-second where the wood is deciding whether to give way or hold fast. In alder, it always gives way.

This is the "drinking" sensation. As I make my first crescent cut, shaving away a sliver of the center, the wood pulls the blade in. It is a tactile suction. You feel the grain fibers parting, not snapping. If you push too hard, you lose control; the knife plows a trench too deep, and you have to fight for the surface back. The alder wants to be empty. It wants to become a cup. The challenge is not making it hollow; the challenge is stopping before it becomes a hole.

I work in layers. A spoon bowl is not carved in one sweep; it is a series of diminishing craters. I start with a V-shaped groove down the center, establishing the depth. Then I widen the walls, angling the blade so the cut is shallow and broad. The shavings come off in translucent curls, thin enough to see the light through them, darkening to a reddish-brown at the center where the wood was densest.

There is a specific weight to this process. My hand is not just moving the knife; it is feeling the center of gravity shift. As the wood disappears, the spoon gets lighter, but it also gets top-heavy. I am balancing two opposing forces: the need for depth to hold soup, and the need for mass in the handle to keep the thing from tipping in the bowl. If the bowl is too deep, the spoon feels like a ladle—clumsy, heavy in the tip. If it is too shallow, it is a spoon for dust, useless for substance.

I look for the "sweet spot," a depth of about eighteen millimeters. At this point, the bowl should feel substantial in the palm but light on the tongue. I run my thumb across the curve of the bowl I am forming. It should feel like a small valley, a landscape that guides liquid to the center without creating a reservoir that is hard to pour. The alder is damp now, sticky with its own oils. It feels alive under the blade, warm from the friction.

The most satisfying moment is when the walls of the bowl become thin enough to flex. I press my thumb inside the rim, testing the give. A good spoon bowl should have a slight spring to it, a resilience. If it is stiff, I have left too much wood; the spoon will feel rigid and industrial. If it is flimsy, I have carved too much; it will feel fragile, like a paper cup. I want the "breath" of the wood. I want the sensation that if I lifted this spoon, it would rise with a slight, buoyant hesitation, as if it were half-made of air.

This is where the tactile satisfaction peaks. It is not about the beauty of the curve—though the curve must be smooth, free of chatter marks and ridges. It is about the feedback loop between my thumb and the wood. I can feel the exact thickness of the wall under my nail. I can feel where the wood is thicker on the left side, where my hand drifted, and I shave a micron off the right side to compensate. The alder drinks the pressure of my correction. It evens out.

I am not building a spoon; I am revealing the one that was already there, hiding in the stick. The alder is cooperative. It forgives small mistakes. It holds the water. And when I finally lift the tool away and run my tongue across the inside curve, I taste the sap, the earth, and the precise, perfect balance of weight and depth. It is a small, dark cup, ready to hold the world.
